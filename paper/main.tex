\begin{abstract}
We give explicit colorings establishing
\[
  W(7,3)>344
\]
and
\[
\begin{gathered}
  w(2;3,32)>1011,\quad w(2;3,33)>1069,\quad w(2;3,35)>1205,\\
  w(2;3,36)>1259,\quad w(2;3,39)>1420.
\end{gathered}
\]
Here and throughout, notation is \emph{color first}: \(W(r,k)\) concerns
\(r\) colors and monochromatic \(k\)-term arithmetic progressions, while
\(w(r;k_1,\ldots,k_r)\) assigns forbidden length \(k_i\) to color \(i\).
The first result is therefore a 7-coloring of \(\interval{344}\) with no
monochromatic 3-term progression, not a 3-coloring avoiding 7-term
progressions.  The mixed results are 2-colorings in which color 1 avoids
3-term progressions and color 2 avoids \(t\)-term progressions.

The search combined focused stochastic local search with clause-weight
redistribution and phase-seeded conflict-driven clause learning.  Most mixed
improvements were obtained by extending existing certificates, while one
required a two-variable coordinated repair found by a SAT solver.  Every
claim is represented by a complete coloring and checked by a small,
standalone verifier that shares no code with the search programs.  We also
describe the role of large-language-model assistants in literature triage,
software development, and experiment orchestration.  Generated prose or
solver status was never treated as evidence: the governing rule was
\emph{certificate or nothing}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

For positive integers \(r\) and \(k\), van der Waerden's theorem says that a
sufficiently long interval, under every \(r\)-coloring, contains a
monochromatic arithmetic progression of length \(k\).  Determining the least
such interval length is difficult even for small parameters.  Computational
lower bounds are naturally constructive: one exhibits a coloring of a long
interval that avoids all forbidden progressions.

Explicit constructions, local search, and propositional satisfiability have
all contributed to this program
\cite{herwig2007,ahmed2014,heule2017,monroe2022,komkov2020}.  Ahmed, Kullmann,
and Snevily studied the mixed family \(w(2;3,t)\), proving
\(w(2;3,19)=349\) and publishing lower-bound certificates through \(t=39\)
\cite{ahmed2014}.  Komkov later published SAT-derived certificates for
several classical parameters, including \(W(7,3)>343\)
\cite{komkov2020}.  The constructions in this paper improve five of the
mixed bounds stated by Ahmed--Kullmann--Snevily and the \(W(7,3)\) bound
stated by Komkov.

The contributions are:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]
  \item six complete, verifier-accepted colorings giving the bounds in the
        abstract;
  \item a hybrid search pipeline in which stochastic local search supplies
        strong phase information to a complete SAT solver;
  \item a small code-independent verifier, independent test generation, and
        cryptographic hashes for the certificates; and
  \item a transparent account of an interactive human--AI computational
        mathematics workflow and its epistemic safeguards.
\end{enumerate}

The inequalities themselves do not depend on a novelty search: each follows
from its displayed finite certificate.  Priority is a separate bibliographic
claim.  To the best of our literature search, the bounds improve the cited
published baselines; the companion repository preserves the source
transcriptions used in that comparison.

The priority audit was last updated on July 23, 2026.  It checked the
Ahmed--Kullmann--Snevily and Komkov source versions used for the baselines,
Monroe's version-7 tables, the Rabung--Lotts construction paper, the
\emph{Leaps in Bounds} tracker, and exact-formula and title searches across
arXiv and the web through that date
\cite{ahmed2014,komkov2020,monroe2022,rabung2012,leaps2026}.  This records the
scope of the priority claim without making certificate validity depend on
bibliographic completeness.

\section{Notation and the physical meaning of the claims}
\label{sec:notation}

An arithmetic progression of length \(k\), abbreviated \(k\ap\), is
\[
  a,\ a+d,\ \ldots,\ a+(k-1)d
\]
with \(d\geq 1\).  Write \(\interval{n}=\{1,\ldots,n\}\).

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \(W(r,k)\) is the least \(n\) such that every coloring
        \(c:\interval{n}\to\{1,\ldots,r\}\) contains a monochromatic
        \(k\ap\).
  \item \(w(r;k_1,\ldots,k_r)\) is the least \(n\) such that every such
        coloring has, for some color \(i\), a \(k_i\ap\) entirely in color
        \(i\).
\end{itemize}

Thus \(W(r,k)=w(r;k,\ldots,k)\).  This paper always puts the number of colors
first.  Some literature reverses the two arguments of the classical
quantity.  Under that alternative convention, our \(W(7,3)\) can be written
\(W(3,7)\).  To make an argument-order mistake impossible to hide, we pair
every formula with its physical object:

\begin{quote}
\textbf{\(W(7,3)>344\) means a 7-coloring of \(\interval{344}\) containing
no monochromatic 3-term arithmetic progression.}
\end{quote}

A concrete collision is worth spelling out.  In length-first notation,
\(W(7,3)\) denotes the quantity written here as \(W(3,7)\): three colors
avoiding 7-term progressions.  Its known lower bound
\(W(3,7)>48{,}811\) is unrelated to the 7-color, 3-term result of this paper
\cite{rabung2012,leaps2026}.

Likewise, \(w(2;3,32)>1011\) means a 2-coloring of
\(\interval{1011}\) in which color 1 has no \(3\ap\) and color 2 has no
\(32\ap\).  The standalone verifier prints both the parameter tuple and the
color-first formula, and regression tests lock this wording.

\section{Certified results}
\label{sec:results}

\begin{theorem}
\label{thm:main}
There is a 7-coloring of \(\interval{344}\) with no monochromatic
\(3\ap\).  There are also 2-colorings of \(\interval{n_t}\) in which color
1 contains no \(3\ap\) and color 2 contains no \(t\ap\), for
\[
  (t,n_t)\in
  \{(32,1011),(33,1069),(35,1205),(36,1259),(39,1420)\}.
\]
Consequently, in color-first notation,
\[
\begin{split}
W(7,3)&>344,\\
w(2;3,32)&>1011,\qquad w(2;3,33)>1069,\\
w(2;3,35)&>1205,\qquad w(2;3,36)>1259,\\
w(2;3,39)&>1420.
\end{split}
\]
\end{theorem}

\section{Certificates and verification}
\label{sec:verification}

\subsection{Certificate format}

A certificate is a whitespace-separated sequence
\[
  r;\quad k_1,\ldots,k_r;\quad n;\quad c_1,\ldots,c_n,
\]
where \(c_j\in\{1,\ldots,r\}\) is the color of \(j\).  Comment lines are
allowed, but the verifier rejects missing colors, extra integer tokens,
out-of-range colors, and malformed parameters.  Exit status 0 means valid,
1 means a forbidden progression was found, and 2 means malformed input.

\subsection{Exhaustive checker}

For each possible first element \(a\), the verifier takes \(i=c_a\), sets
\(k=k_i\), and enumerates
\[
  1\leq d\leq \left\lfloor\frac{n-a}{k-1}\right\rfloor.
\]
It rejects if all of
\[
  c_a,c_{a+d},\ldots,c_{a+(k-1)d}
\]
equal \(i\).

\begin{lemma}
\label{lem:verifier}
If the verifier accepts a certificate with parameters
\((r;k_1,\ldots,k_r;n)\), then the certificate establishes
\(w(r;k_1,\ldots,k_r)>n\).
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Suppose a forbidden monochromatic progression exists.  Let \(a\) be its
first term, \(d\) its common difference, and \(i=c_a\) its color.  Because
the progression lies in \(\interval n\), the verifier's loop at \(a\)
includes this \(d\).  It then checks exactly \(k_i\) terms and finds all of
them colored \(i\), so it rejects.  The contrapositive gives the claim.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of \Cref{thm:main}]
The six complete colorings named in \Cref{tab:certificates} are included in
the companion repository.  Each was accepted by the standalone verifier at
the following commit:
\begin{center}
\small\texttt{bd16953d517f57935574b15d60f7c5715a9d5c75}
\end{center}
Each file matches the SHA-256 digest in that table.  Apply
\Cref{lem:verifier}.
\end{proof}

Implementation independence is intentional: \texttt{verifier.c} shares no
code with a generator, searcher, model decoder, or source-expansion tool.
An independent Python program generates eight small regression instances
covering valid, invalid, mixed-length, swapped-length, malformed, and
\(k=2\) cases.  The repository's full check also regenerates 12 published
construction certificates byte for byte, accepts all 34 tracked complete
certificates, and validates the certificate hash manifest.  These tests do
not replace the soundness argument above, but they reduce the risk of a
parser or implementation error.

\section{Search encodings and pipeline}
\label{sec:search}

\subsection{Implicit Boolean encoding for
\texorpdfstring{\(w(2;3,t)\)}{w(2;3,t)}}

Let \(x_j=1\) mean that \(j\) has color 1, the color that must avoid
\(3\ap\)s.  Every \(3\ap\), \((a,b,c)\), induces the negative clause
\[
  \lnot x_a\lor\lnot x_b\lor\lnot x_c.
\]
Every \(t\ap\), \((a_1,\ldots,a_t)\), induces the positive clause
\[
  x_{a_1}\lor\cdots\lor x_{a_t}.
\]
The searcher stores progressions and their incidence with elements, but does
not materialize a general-purpose SAT object on its hot path.  It maintains
violation counts incrementally after a flip.

\subsection{Uniform encoding for
\texorpdfstring{\(W(7,3)\)}{W(7,3)}}

For the 7-color instance, variables \(y_{j,q}\) encode that element \(j\)
has color \(q\).  Exactly-one constraints are imposed for every element,
and every \(3\ap\) \((a,b,c)\) and color \(q\) contributes
\[
  \lnot y_{a,q}\lor\lnot y_{b,q}\lor\lnot y_{c,q}.
\]
Search states use a direct one-color-per-element representation; the
one-hot encoding is emitted only for the CDCL lane.

\subsection{Focused local search}

The stochastic lane combines mixed random-walk ideas
\cite{selman1994} with DDFW-style redistribution of clause weights
\cite{ishtaiwi2005}.  A step focuses on a currently violated progression,
scores recolorings using the affected incidence lists, and makes either a
greedy or noisy move.  Restarts include published-certificate seeds,
reversals, cyclic and residue templates, palindromic instances, and random
starts.  Independent workers explore different restart classes and seeds.
Best-so-far states are explicitly marked as \emph{not certificates}.

\subsection{Phase-seeded CDCL}

Local search often reached assignments with one violated progression but
could not coordinate the several flips needed to escape.  We therefore
emitted a conventional CNF while polarity-transforming each primary variable
so that the solver's default phase represents the supplied search state.
Kissat 4.0.4 \cite{kissat2024} was used for the decisive full-instance runs;
CaDiCaL 3.0.1 \cite{biere2024} provided an API lane for Hamming-ball, fixed-window,
reflection, and small-instance cross-checks.  Kissat and CaDiCaL are
candidate generators only.  A decoded model is not a result until the
standalone verifier accepts it.

Reflection lex leaders were used only where their safety followed from the
unseeded symmetry.  Palindromic and fixed-window constraints were treated as
streamlining: satisfiable output still gives an unconditional lower bound,
but an unsatisfiable streamlined instance says nothing about the unrestricted
problem.  No unsatisfiability claim is made in this paper.

\section{How the six certificates were found}
\label{sec:case-studies}

\subsection{The \texorpdfstring{\(W(7,3)\)}{W(7,3)} ``wall''}

Starting from Komkov's coloring at \(n=343\), repeated DDFW runs at
\(n=344\) reached assignments with a single violation.  Across independent
basins, that violation was always
\[
  \{2,173,344\},
\]
the \(3\ap\) with common difference 171.  This repeated obstruction was
useful phase information, not evidence of impossibility.

Unseeded Kissat did not settle even the known-satisfiable \(n=343\) encoding
within ten minutes in our calibration.  With seed-relative polarities, the
same calibration completed in under one second.  A phase-seeded run from one
of the one-violation \(n=344\) basins then returned a model.  The accepted
coloring differs from that basin at 25 positions, distributed across the
interval, and recolors element 344 itself.  The experiment therefore
supports a specific hybrid account: local search located a strong basin, and
CDCL assembled a globally coordinated escape that single-flip search had not
found.

At \(n=345\), two further local-search chains reached the same remaining
violation \(\{3,174,345\}\), also with difference 171, but bounded
phase-seeded CDCL runs returned no decision.  This is reported only as a
reproducible search observation.  It is neither evidence of unsatisfiability
nor a determination of \(W(7,3)\).

\subsection{The mixed family}

The nine compact certificates for \(t=31,\ldots,39\) were transcribed from
the Ahmed--Kullmann--Snevily appendix, mechanically expanded, and accepted
by the standalone verifier before use as seeds.  For \(t=32,33,36,39\),
orientation and endpoint-color sweeps produced short extension ladders.
Several steps required no interior change; one \(t=33\) step required a
single local-search flip.  The final accepted lengths were 1011, 1069, 1259,
and 1420 respectively.

The \(t=35\) step illustrates the value of the CDCL handoff.  A reversed
seed at \(n=1205\) remained one violation away under the endpoint probe.
Three phase-seeded Kissat configurations returned the same satisfying model
in 0.05--0.06 seconds.  Relative to the basin, the model flips exactly
elements 892 and 1188.  That two-variable coordination was unavailable to
the one-flip endpoint repair, and all three decoded copies independently
passed the verifier.

Bounded searches for the next lengths and for \(t=31,34,37,38\) were
undecided.  In particular, a timeout is recorded as \textsc{unknown}, never
as unsatisfiable.

\section{Human--AI workflow and epistemic controls}
\label{sec:ai}

Recent systems have coupled machine learning or large language models with
mathematical evaluators, program search, or human interpretation
\cite{davies2021,romeraparedes2024,novikov2025}.  The workflow here was less
autonomous and more conversational.  OpenAI Codex and Anthropic Claude were
used interactively for literature triage, experiment design, C and Python
implementation, process supervision, code review, and log synthesis.  Google
Gemini was used during a manuscript-stage check that exposed the danger of
reversing color-first and length-first notation.

The human researcher chose the target family, set compute budgets, approved
external actions, redirected unsuccessful work, and decided what evidence
could support a public claim.  The language-model systems proposed and
implemented many of the concrete search steps.  They are acknowledged as
research tools rather than listed as authors because they cannot take
responsibility for the manuscript or its claims.

Four controls separated useful assistance from mathematical evidence:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Generator--verifier separation.}  Search code and the
        verifier share no implementation.
  \item \textbf{Complete witnesses.}  A score, a nearly satisfying state,
        an LLM assertion, or a SAT timeout is not a result.
  \item \textbf{Persistent provenance.}  Contemporaneous logs record failed
        approaches, corrections, solver versions, seeds, and bounded
        \textsc{unknown} outcomes.
  \item \textbf{Notation redundancy.}  Formulas are paired with plain
        descriptions of the physical coloring, and verifier output repeats
        the color count and forbidden lengths before printing a formula.
\end{enumerate}

This differs from FunSearch \cite{romeraparedes2024} and AlphaEvolve
\cite{novikov2025}: no learned or evolutionary controller maintained a
population of programs.  Instead, conversational agents helped build and
operate a conventional, domain-specific SLS--SAT pipeline.  The common
principle is evaluator grounding.  Here the evaluator is deliberately small,
deterministic, and separate from every heuristic component.

\section{Reproducibility}
\label{sec:reproducibility}

The companion repository is \repo.  At the verifier revision used for this
paper, a verification-only reproduction requires a C11 compiler, POSIX
\texttt{sh}, \texttt{make}, and Python 3:
\begin{verbatim}
make verifier
./verifier certs/RECORD_W7_3_gt344.txt
./verifier certs/m5_w2_3_32_gt1011.txt
make check
\end{verbatim}
The final command rebuilds dependency-free programs, regenerates independent
test fixtures, runs the verifier and incremental-state tests, reproduces
published-source certificates, verifies all tracked certificates, and checks
their SHA-256 digests.  The intermediate accepted certificates in the
successful \(t=32,33,36,39\) extension ladders are also tracked under
\path{certs/} and covered by the same command.

Search binaries were compiled with C99/C11 compilers and hot runs used
\texttt{-O3 -march=native}; a profile-guided build was also exercised for
the mixed local-search lane.  These optimizations affect search speed, not
certificate meaning.  CaDiCaL and Kissat are not required for verification.
The exact solver releases used for the final search lane were CaDiCaL 3.0.1
and Kissat 4.0.4 (release commit \texttt{8af8e56}); the latter passed its
bundled test suite before use.

\section{Limitations and open questions}

The paper establishes lower bounds only.  It supplies no proof of
unsatisfiability at the next interval length and therefore no exact value.
The repeated common-difference-171 obstruction near \(W(7,3)\) is an
empirical feature of sampled basins, not a theorem about all colorings.
Likewise, unsuccessful mixed-family searches do not rank the true difficulty
of the remaining parameters.

The verifier is small and tested but not formally verified.  Its source,
independent fixtures, complete witnesses, and hashes are supplied so that
others can inspect it, write a second checker, or verify the progressions
directly.  An archival release should freeze the paper, source, certificates,
and manifest together.

Finally, the literature search and manuscript benefited from fallible
language models.  This is a reason for stronger bibliographic and notation
checks, not a reason to weaken them.  The certificate claims are independent
of those tools; the priority claim remains subject to ordinary scholarly
correction if an earlier certificate is located.
